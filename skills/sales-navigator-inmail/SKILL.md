# Sales Navigator InMail

Nash supplies (1) the recipient's LinkedIn profile (PDF export, screenshot or pasted text) and (2) a short note on his purpose (any topic: mentor ask, customer interview, recruiting, research, networking). Produce a ready-to-send InMail: subject line + body.

## Step 1: Read before writing

- Read the whole profile: headline, About, Experience, Education, recent posts/activity, mutual connections.
- Pick the 1-2 profile facts most relevant to Nash's purpose. These become the hook. Prefer something specific and current (a recent post, current research, a past role that matches the ask) over generic flattery.
- If Nash's purpose references a project doc or earlier context in the conversation, pull the problem statement from there. Do not invent facts, metrics or traction. Leave out claims the source itself flags as unverified.
- If the purpose is too vague to write a specific ask, ask Nash one short question before drafting.

## Step 2: Subject line

- Hard limit: 200 characters. Target under 60 so it is not truncated in the inbox.
- Give 3 options, recommend one and say why in one line.
- Make it specific to the recipient: name the shared context (event, school, their topic) or turn their work into a question. Avoid generic subjects like "Quick question" or "Reaching out".

## Step 3: Body

Hard limit: 1,900 characters. Target 900-1,500. Count characters (run a quick count in Bash if unsure) and report the count.

Structure:
1. **Greeting + who Nash is** (1-2 sentences). InMail is a cold first contact, so state identity and shared context up front (e.g. Berkeley exchange student, event/team name, how he found them) so it does not read as a sales pitch.
2. **Why them** woven into the context: reference the hook from Step 1.
3. **The problem / what Nash is working on** (2-4 sentences, plain language, no jargon dump).
4. **The ask**: 1-2 concrete questions tied to the recipient's expertise, numbered if two. Each question should be one only this person is well placed to answer.
5. **Call to action**: small and time-bounded (e.g. "10 to 15 minutes before [date]"), plus flexibility ("Happy to work around your schedule").
6. **Sign-off**: "Thanks,\nNash".
7. **Optional P.S.** only if Nash asks: e.g. connection limit note: "P.S. I've hit LinkedIn's weekly invitation limit, so I couldn't send a connection request. I'd genuinely appreciate it if you'd like to connect, or I'll send one next week!"

## Style rules

- English, warm but direct. Short paragraphs.
- No em dashes. No emoji.
- No hype, no overclaiming, no fabricated numbers.
- Fix Nash's English if he supplies wording (e.g. "excited" vs "exciting", "genuinely" vs "generally"), and briefly say what was fixed and why.

## Output format

Reply in Chinese with English terms where useful (bilingual). Include:
1. 3 subject options with one recommended (one-line reason).
2. The full body in a quote block, ready to paste.
3. Character count (body, and subject if near limit).
4. 1-3 short notes on choices: which profile fact was used as the hook and why, anything deliberately left out (e.g. unverified metrics), and any faster channel worth trying (e.g. event Slack).

If Nash asks for a connection-request note instead, the limit is 300 characters and there is no subject line.

## Example (abbreviated)

Input: profile of a university researcher who studies AI and human judgement and is mentoring a hackathon; previously in growth at an enterprise AI company. Purpose: get feedback on Nash's hackathon project (an AI agent that proposes code fixes).

Subject (recommended): "Hackathon mentor question: when do engineers trust AI fixes?" (turns their research topic into the question)

Body asks: (1) would engineers trust a small diff with evidence more than a full rewrite, tied to their delegation research; (2) who owns and pays for this, tied to their enterprise sales experience. CTA: 10 to 15 minutes before Demo Day.
