# Impressive Self-Intro (Introduce / Reflect / Relate)

Use this whenever Nash needs to introduce himself to someone in a career context: replying to a recruiter, a LinkedIn outreach, a coffee-chat request, a networking email, or a spoken "tell me about yourself" answer for an interview.

The message itself is written in English. Explanations and review notes to Nash are in Chinese with English terms alongside.

## The framework

Three parts, in order. Each part is one short paragraph (written) or 2 to 3 sentences (spoken).

1. **Introduce**: who he is plus the origin of his motivation.
   > "My name is ___ and ever since ___, I've wanted to do ___..."
   - The "ever since" clause needs a concrete trigger (a moment, a class, a discovery), not an abstract claim. If the trigger is vague, it reads as floating.
   - The "wanted to do" clause should already point toward what the target role does.

2. **Reflect**: two experiences, chained causally.
   > "So, I took it upon myself to ___, where I did ___. This led me to ___, where I did ___..."
   - First experience shows initiative ("took it upon myself").
   - "This led me to" must be a real progression, not two unrelated bullet points glued together.
   - Each "where I did" states a concrete action and outcome, not a title.
   - Pick the two experiences that map to what the target role cares about (e.g. Operations: systems/process + working with people; PM: user insight + building).

3. **Relate**: bridge past to the opportunity.
   > "Now, as I think about what I'd like to do next, I'd love to [combine ___ and ___ / take what I've learned from ___ and apply it to ___ / do more of ___ / focus more on ___], which is what brings me here today."
   - Choose ONE of the four bridge patterns.
   - The "apply it to ___" target must name what the role actually does.
   - Adapt the closing to the medium: "which is what brings me here today" for interviews and in-person; for written messages use something like "which is what makes this [role] so interesting to me" or "which is why your message caught my attention."

## Workflow

1. Identify the context: who the reader is, the role or opportunity, and whether it is written or spoken.
2. Identify what the role cares about (from the job description or recruiter message). Pick the Reflect experiences that map to it.
3. Draft the three parts. For written replies, wrap them with a one-line opener (e.g. "Thank you for reaching out. I would like to learn more about this opportunity.") and a short sign-off ("Looking forward to hearing from you!" / "Best regards, Nash (Wen-Long) Wang").
4. Run the checklist below, then show the draft. For written messages, use a message-compose card if available; for spoken answers, show it as text and note the target length (about 60 to 90 seconds).
5. After the draft, give at most 2 to 3 short notes in Chinese: what to verify, and any optional tweak. No long explanations.

## Content rules (learned from Nash's feedback)

- **Cut low-value logistics.** Do not include school name, year of study, academic-year dates or program details unless the reader specifically needs them. The framework carries the story; credentials can come later.
- **Do not add logistics questions** (full-time vs internship, locations, sponsorship, "happy to set up a call") into the self-intro unless Nash asks for them. If there is a real gating issue (e.g. work authorization), mention it to Nash separately in the notes, not in the message.
- **Every claim must be true at the level stated.** Flag verbs that may overstate ("lead" vs "run" vs "conduct" vs "help with"). Never invent coaching, management, or metrics he did not state.
- **No em dashes** in the message. No emoji.
- Keep it tight: a recruiter reply is roughly 120 to 180 words.

## Example (recruiter reply to an operations manager role, final version Nash approved)

> Hi [Recruiter],
>
> Thank you for reaching out. I would like to learn more about this opportunity.
>
> My name is Nash, and ever since I learned that the best outcomes happen when everyone's incentives line up, I've wanted to build teams and systems where that actually happens.
>
> So, I took it upon myself to join a research lab as an undergraduate research assistant, where I built tools that automate data extraction from semiconductor companies' annual reports and turned a manual process into one that runs reliably at scale. This led me to join a startup team at UC Berkeley, where I lead B2B customer interviews and outreach, learning how operators work day to day and where their processes break down.
>
> Now, as I think about what I'd like to do next, I'd love to take what I've learned from building data-driven systems and listening to frontline operators, and apply it to leading a team on the ground, which is what makes this role so interesting to me.
>
> Looking forward to hearing from you!
>
> Best regards,
> Nash (Wen-Long) Wang

## Checklist before showing the draft

- [ ] Introduce has a concrete "ever since" trigger
- [ ] Reflect has two experiences linked by a real "this led me to"
- [ ] Relate uses one bridge pattern and names what the role does
- [ ] Closing line fits the medium (written vs spoken)
- [ ] No school/year/program filler, no logistics questions unless asked
- [ ] Every verb and claim is something Nash actually did
- [ ] No em dashes, no emoji
